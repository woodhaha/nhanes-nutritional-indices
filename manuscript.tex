\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Background \& Aims:} Composite nutritional indices (PNI, CONUT, GNRI) predict mortality in single-cancer cohorts, but cross-cancer comparison and population-level gastrointestinal (GI) validation are lacking. We evaluated these indices across five cancer types with in-depth GI analysis including time-dependent, competing risk, and decomposition analyses.

\textbf{Methods:} We identified 2,942 cancer patients from NHANES III (1988--1994) and Continuous NHANES (2005--2016) with mortality follow-up through 2019. Cox models estimated HR per 1-SD within each cancer type and in pooled analyses. GI-specific analyses included time-dependent Cox, landmark analysis, restricted cubic splines, RMST, competing risks (cause-specific and Fine-Gray), PNI decomposition, and joint inflammatory assessment.

\textbf{Results:} Across 2,942 patients (1,135 deaths), all indices significantly predicted mortality: PNI (HR 0.78, 0.73--0.84), CONUT (HR 0.78, 0.73--0.82), GNRI (HR 0.80, 0.75--0.86). CONUT varied by cancer type (interaction p=0.016). In GI cancer, CONUT and GNRI showed the strongest unadjusted associations (HR 0.71 and 0.67, both p$<$0.001); in survey-weighted analysis only CONUT remained significant (HR 0.76, p=0.003). The PNI effect was strongly time-dependent (0--2yr HR 0.34, interaction p<0.001), operated through non-cancer death (HR 0.63 vs. GI cancer death HR 1.06), and was albumin-driven.

\textbf{Conclusions:} Nutritional indices predict mortality across cancer types, but cancer type moderates CONUT. In GI cancer, CONUT and GNRI independently predict mortality after correction.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Cancer-related malnutrition is a critical challenge across oncology, with prevalence ranging from 30\% to 80\% depending on tumor site, stage, and treatment modality~\cite{muscaritoli2021,arends2017,fearon2011}. Malnutrition is independently associated with reduced treatment tolerance, increased postoperative complications, diminished quality of life, and shorter survival~\cite{muscaritoli2021}. International guidelines, including those from the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) and the Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition (GLIM), recommend routine nutritional screening from the time of cancer diagnosis~\cite{muscaritoli2021,cederholm2019}.

Three composite nutritional indices derived from routine laboratory data have been widely studied as prognostic markers. The Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI), calculated from serum albumin and lymphocyte count, was originally developed to assess perioperative risk in gastrointestinal surgery~\cite{onodera1984}. The Controlling Nutritional Status (CONUT) score integrates albumin, lymphocyte count, and total cholesterol to capture a broader nutritional-inflammatory spectrum~\cite{ignacio2005}. The Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI) incorporates albumin and body weight relative to ideal weight, initially validated in elderly populations~\cite{bouillanne2005}. Each index has been associated with survival in single-cancer cohorts, predominantly from East Asian surgical series~\cite{yang2016,sun2014,pinato2012}.

Three important gaps remain. First, it is unknown whether the prognostic performance of these indices generalizes across cancer types or is context-specific. Most studies examine a single malignancy, and the indices have not been systematically compared within a single, nationally representative population spanning multiple cancer types. Second, even for gastrointestinal (GI) cancer---where malnutrition and cachexia are most clinically salient, affecting up to 80\% of patients~\cite{kapala2025}---population-level validation with comprehensive temporal and mechanistic analysis is lacking. Existing GI cancer studies are predominantly single-center surgical series with limited follow-up and minimal adjustment for survey design or competing risks. Third, the mechanistic pathways through which nutritional indices affect survival remain poorly characterized. Specifically, it is unclear whether these indices capture anti-tumor immunity, physiological reserve and resilience to treatment-related complications, or systemic inflammatory burden, and whether their prognostic value is independent of dietary quality.

To address these gaps, we conducted a two-component analysis leveraging two independent NHANES generations spanning 30 years (NHANES III 1988--1994 and Continuous NHANES 2005--2016) with mortality follow-up through 2019. The first component is a cross-cancer pooled analysis comparing PNI, CONUT, and GNRI across five cancer types (gastrointestinal, breast, female reproductive, prostate/urinary, and other solid tumors) to assess generalizability versus context-specific performance. The second component provides an in-depth GI cancer analysis including time-dependent effects, landmark analysis, competing risks (cause-specific and Fine-Gray), optimal threshold identification, restricted cubic splines, restricted mean survival time, PNI decomposition, joint inflammatory assessment, and comparison with dietary quality (HEI-2015).

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Study Population}

We used data from two independent NHANES cycles: NHANES III (1988--1994) and Continuous NHANES (2005--2016). NHANES is a nationally representative cross-sectional survey of the civilian non-institutionalized US population. The analytic sample included participants aged $\ge$18 years with available nutrition biomarker data (serum albumin, lymphocyte count, total cholesterol, BMI) and linked mortality data. Of 44,066 participants meeting inclusion criteria, 3,586 reported a cancer diagnosis. After excluding 535 with missing nutrition biomarkers or ineligible mortality linkage, and 109 with hematologic malignancies (n=60) or lung cancer (n=49) where site-specific event counts were insufficient, 2,942 cancer patients remained. The GI subset comprised 353 patients.

The NHANES protocol was approved by the NCHS Ethics Review Board. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Because NHANES is a cross-sectional survey, participants are sampled at varying times after cancer diagnosis. This prevalent cohort design introduces length-biased sampling: patients with longer post-diagnosis survival are more likely to be included, which would be expected to bias the observed PNI-mortality association toward the null (i.e., the reported protective effects are likely conservative). Time since diagnosis is unavailable in the public-use data and is not adjusted for.

\subsection{Cancer Type Classification}

Cancer diagnosis was ascertained from self-reported medical conditions questionnaires (MCQ230A/MCQ240A for continuous NHANES; HAC3OS for NHANES III). Cancer types were classified into five pancancer groups: gastrointestinal (GI: esophagus, stomach, colon, rectum, pancreas, liver, gallbladder), breast, female reproductive (cervix, ovary, uterine), prostate/urinary (prostate, bladder, kidney, testicular), and other solid tumors (melanoma, thyroid, brain, bone, head/neck, skin).

\subsection{Nutritional Indices}

PNI was calculated as $10 \times \text{serum albumin (g/dL)} + 0.005 \times \text{lymphocyte count} (\mu\text{L}^{-1})$~\cite{onodera1984}. CONUT was derived as the sum of three component scores: albumin ($\ge$3.5 g/dL = 0, 3.0--3.49 = 2, 2.5--2.99 = 4, $<$2.5 = 6), lymphocyte count ($\ge$1600 $\mu$L$^{-1}$ = 0, 1200--1599 = 1, 800--1199 = 2, $<$800 = 3), and total cholesterol ($\ge$180 mg/dL = 0, 140--179 = 1, 100--139 = 2, $<$100 = 3)~\cite{ignacio2005}. GNRI was calculated as $14.89 \times \text{albumin (g/dL)} + 41.7 \times (\text{BMI}/22)$~\cite{bouillanne2005}.

HEI-2015 was calculated from 24-hour dietary recall data using the Food Patterns Equivalents Database. Serum albumin was measured using the bromcresol purple method in Continuous NHANES and bromcresol green in NHANES III, with cohort included as a fixed effect to account for assay differences. To align hazard ratio direction across indices, CONUT values were multiplied by $-$1 prior to standardization so that higher values uniformly indicate better nutritional status; PNI and GNRI were used in their original orientation (higher = better).

\subsection{Mortality and Statistical Analysis}

Mortality status was ascertained through NCHS National Death Index through December 31, 2019. Cause of death was classified using UCOD\_LEADING (code 002 = malignant neoplasm). In GI cancer, cause-specific death was proxied by baseline GI cancer plus UCOD=002.

\subsubsection{Cross-Cancer Analysis}

Cox proportional hazards models estimated HR per 1-SD within each cancer type, adjusted for age, race/ethnicity, education, and income. Sex was excluded from cancer-type-specific models given sex-specific cancer types (breast, prostate, female reproductive). Across-cancer pooled estimates were obtained from a stratified Cox model with cancer type as strata. Cancer type $\times$ index interactions were tested using likelihood ratio tests. Bonferroni correction: $\alpha=0.0033$ for 15 tests (5 types $\times$ 3 indices). The primary cross-cancer models were unweighted because the estimand of interest is the covariate-adjusted association within and across cancer types rather than a population-average effect; survey-weighted sensitivity analyses using NHANES design variables and sampling weights yielded consistent pooled estimates.

\subsubsection{GI-Specific Analysis}

Cox models were fitted with crude and adjusted (age, sex, race/ethnicity) specifications. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals; time-dependent Cox models with 2- and 5-year splits addressed PH violations. Landmark analyses at 1, 3, and 5 years examined conditional survival. Maximally selected log-rank statistics identified the optimal PNI threshold~\cite{han2022}; restricted mean survival time (RMST) at 10 years was estimated~\cite{royston2013}. Restricted cubic splines (3 knots) examined nonlinearity.

Cause-specific Cox and Fine-Gray subdistribution models addressed competing risks (GI cancer death vs. non-cancer death)~\cite{putter2007,finegray1999}. PNI was decomposed into albumin alone and lymphocyte alone, comparing AIC, change in C-statistic (2,000 bootstrap replications), and likelihood ratio tests. E-values quantified robustness to unmeasured confounding~\cite{vanderweele2017}. Survey-weighted Cox models used NHANES design variables (SDMVPSU, SDMVSTRA, WTMEC2YR). Bonferroni correction: $\alpha=0.0083$ for 6 GI tests (3 indices $\times$ 2 adjustment levels). Additional exploratory analyses (time-dependent effects, landmark, competing risks, RCS, decomposition, inflammatory markers, and dietary comparisons) are reported without correction for multiple testing and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Missing data for the primary exposure and covariate variables were minimal (<5\% for albumin, lymphocyte, cholesterol, and BMI) and handled by complete-case analysis. Analyses used R 4.6.0 with \texttt{survival} and \texttt{survey} packages. Full analysis code and a session info file listing package versions are available at \url{https://github.com/woodhaha/nhanes-nutritional-indices}.

\subsubsection{Baseline Characteristics}

Baseline characteristics differed substantially across cancer types (Table~1). GI patients had the highest mortality (53.5\%) and highest CONUT scores (mean 1.08). Prostate/urinary patients were oldest (71.4 years) with the highest proportion of males (94.0\%). Female reproductive cancer patients were youngest (57.2 years) with the lowest mortality (29.2\%).

\subsubsection{GI Cancer Is Uniquely Associated With Severe Nutritional Depletion}

The systematic superiority of nutritional indices in GI cancer was grounded in baseline nutritional disparity. GI cancer patients had the highest all-cause mortality (53.5\%) and the widest PNI survival gradient (low vs. high PNI tertile: 5.8 vs. 10.6 years median survival; difference 4.8 years), whereas the gradient was minimal or absent in breast ($-$0.8 years), prostate/urinary ($-$0.3 years), and female reproductive (1.0 years) cancers. This pattern is consistent with the known gradient of nutritional risk across cancer types: cachexia affects up to 80\% of GI malignancies compared with 9\% of breast and 5\% of prostate cancer~\cite{kapala2025}, indicating that the prognostic discrimination of nutritional indices is proportional to the underlying malnutrition burden of each cancer type.

\subsubsection{Cancer Type Interaction and Pooled Analysis}

In the cancer-type-stratified pooled analysis, all three indices robustly predicted all-cause mortality (PNI: HR 0.78, CONUT: HR 0.78, GNRI: HR 0.80; all p$<$0.001). CONUT demonstrated significant effect modification by cancer type (LRT $\chi^2=12.15$, df=4, p=0.016), driven by stronger associations in GI and other solid tumors and null effects in breast and prostate/urinary cancers. Neither PNI (LRT p=0.23) nor GNRI (p=0.23) showed significant interaction, though the modest sample size within each cancer type limits statistical power for detecting interaction effects.

In survey-weighted sensitivity analysis (Continuous NHANES subset, n=2,669, 918 events), pooled results were confirmed: PNI HR 0.74 (95\% CI 0.64--0.86, p$<$0.001), CONUT HR 0.79 (0.72--0.86, p$<$0.001), GNRI HR 0.86 (0.77--0.97, p=0.012). E-values for the pooled estimates were 1.87 (PNI), 1.90 (CONUT), and 1.80 (GNRI), indicating modest robustness to unmeasured confounding. These values are lower than typical E-values for established risk factors such as smoking (typically $>$2.5), underscoring that residual confounding by unmeasured factors such as cancer stage cannot be ruled out.

\subsection{Part 2: In-Depth GI Cancer Analysis}

\subsubsection{All-Cause Mortality}

After adjustment, GNRI showed the strongest unadjusted association (HR 0.67, 95\% CI 0.57--0.80, p$<$0.001), followed by CONUT (HR 0.71, 0.61--0.83, p$<$0.001) and PNI (HR 0.78, 0.63--0.96, p=0.020). After Bonferroni correction ($\alpha=0.0083$), CONUT and GNRI remained significant while PNI did not. However, in survey-weighted models restricted to the Continuous NHANES subset, only CONUT remained significant (HR 0.76, 95\% CI 0.63--0.91, p=0.003), whereas PNI (HR 0.87, 0.61--1.23, p=0.432) and GNRI (HR 0.80, 0.61--1.05, p=0.103) were attenuated (Table~3).

\subsubsection{Time-Dependent Effects}

The proportional hazards assumption was violated for PNI (Schoenfeld p=0.017) but not for CONUT (p=0.430) or GNRI (p=0.087). Time-dependent Cox models revealed marked attenuation of the PNI effect: the protective association was strongest in the first 2 years (HR 0.34, 95\% CI 0.21--0.54, p$<$0.001) and non-significant thereafter (2--5 year: HR 1.32, p=0.490; 5+ year: HR 0.81, p=0.450; interaction p$<$0.001). GNRI showed a similar temporal pattern (0--2 year HR 0.53, p=0.034), while CONUT demonstrated more stable effects across periods.

In landmark analysis, PNI showed attenuated and non-significant effects at 1, 3, and 5 years, while CONUT and GNRI maintained significant associations at all landmark times (Table~4). In sensitivity analyses excluding deaths within 2 years, PNI was no longer associated with mortality (HR 0.98, 95\% CI 0.78--1.23, p=0.830), confirming that its prognostic effect is concentrated in the early post-assessment period. In contrast, CONUT and GNRI maintained significant associations (HR 0.77, p=0.006 and HR 0.70, p$<$0.001, respectively), suggesting more stable long-term prognostic value.

\subsubsection{Optimal PNI Threshold and Dose-Response}

Maximally selected log-rank statistics identified PNI $<$ 48.5 as the optimal prognostic threshold (p$<.0001). Patients below this threshold had markedly shorter median survival (6.6 vs. 12.8 years). RMST analysis at 10 years confirmed a clinically meaningful difference of 1.97 years between groups (95\% CI 1.19--2.76, p$<$0.001). The population attributable fraction, calculated under the causal assumption that the PNI-mortality association is unconfounded, was 13.4\% (95\% CI 6.2\%--20.1\%). Restricted cubic spline analysis confirmed a nonlinear relationship for PNI (nonlinearity p$<.0001) with sharp risk increase below approximately 45 and a plateau above 50, while CONUT and GNRI did not significantly deviate from linearity (nonlinearity p=0.110 and p=0.160, respectively).

\subsubsection{Competing Risks Analysis}

Cause-specific Cox models revealed divergent patterns across indices (Table~5). PNI primarily protected against non-cancer death (HR 0.63, 95\% CI 0.48--0.83, p=0.001) rather than GI cancer-specific death (HR 1.06, 0.78--1.45, p=0.700). CONUT and GNRI showed protective effects against both cancer-specific and non-cancer death. Fine-Gray subdistribution models confirmed these patterns, with no index reaching significance for GI cancer death (PNI sHR 1.16, p=0.490; CONUT sHR 0.82, p=0.240; GNRI sHR 0.79, p=0.120) and GNRI remaining significant for non-cancer death (sHR 0.80, p=0.039).

\subsubsection{PNI Decomposition and Inflammatory Markers}

PNI decomposition revealed that serum albumin alone (HR 0.69, 95\% CI 0.58--0.82) outperformed the composite PNI across multiple metrics: lower AIC (1595.8 vs. 1607.8), larger bootstrap C-statistic improvement ($\Delta$C-stat +0.032, 95\% CI 0.010--0.063 vs. +0.022, CI $-$0.001--0.051), and significantly better fit (likelihood ratio test p$<$0.001). Lymphocyte count alone provided negligible discrimination ($\Delta$C-stat +0.002, 95\% CI $-$0.007--0.014; LRT p=0.460).

In the subset with neutrophil and platelet counts (n=258, 125 events), NLR (HR 1.43, 95\% CI 1.19--1.72, p$<$0.001) and SII (HR 1.30, 1.10--1.54, p=0.002) significantly predicted mortality. C-statistic improvement was comparable across indices (+0.017 for PNI, +0.017 for logNLR). In mutually adjusted models including both PNI and logNLR, neither remained significant (PNI HR 0.86, p=0.290; logNLR HR 1.22, p=0.090), indicating shared predictive information.

The joint PNI $\times$ NLR analysis identified a particularly high-risk group: Low PNI + High NLR (HR 1.67, 95\% CI 1.06--2.63, p=0.028) compared to the High PNI + Low NLR reference. RERI was $-$0.97 (95\% CI $-$2.61 to $-$0.10). The wide confidence interval (spanning 2.5 units) limits precision of this estimate, and the direction is sensitive to the choice of reference group, precluding strong conclusions about interaction on the additive scale.

Among 250 patients with CRP measurements, CRP was non-significantly associated with mortality (HR 1.18 per log-SD, p=0.190). No PNI $\times$ CRP interaction was observed (p=0.780). The CALLY index (albumin $\times$ lymphocyte / CRP) did not outperform PNI (HR 0.85, p=0.200; C-stat 0.659 vs. 0.667 for PNI).

\subsubsection{Additional Sensitivity Analyses}

Survey-weighted Cox models in the GI subset (Continuous NHANES only, n=270, 134 events) confirmed CONUT as robust (HR 0.76, 95\% CI 0.63--0.91, p=0.003), while PNI (HR 0.87, 95\% CI 0.61--1.23, p=0.432) and GNRI (HR 0.80, 95\% CI 0.61--1.05, p=0.103) were attenuated and no longer significant. E-values for the primary GI analysis ranged from 1.66 (PNI) to 1.97 (GNRI), indicating limited robustness: an unmeasured confounder with a risk ratio of approximately 1.7--2.0 (readily plausible for cancer stage) could explain the PNI association.

Cohort-stratified analyses revealed consistent effects across NHANES generations: PNI showed similar associations in NHANES III (HR 0.69, p=0.015) and Continuous NHANES (HR 0.75, p=0.047), with no significant cohort-by-PNI interaction (p=0.480). Similar consistency was observed for CONUT (p=0.940) and GNRI (p=0.950).

Adjustment for additional socioeconomic covariates (education, income) and further adjustment for smoking and Charlson comorbidity in the subset with available data did not attenuate the associations: PNI HR 0.68 (95\% CI 0.48--0.97, p=0.035), CONUT HR 0.63 (95\% CI 0.50--0.80, p$<$0.001), GNRI HR 0.72 (95\% CI 0.55--0.93, p=0.013).

The survival gradient across PNI tertiles was most pronounced in GI cancer (median survival 5.8 vs. 10.6 years in low vs. high tertile), whereas it was minimal or absent in breast and prostate/urinary cancers, consistent with PNI's greater discriminative value in malnutrition-prone populations.
\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Summary of Principal Findings}

This study yields three principal findings. First, the prognostic performance of nutritional indices varies across cancer types in a pattern consistent with the underlying nutritional-inflammatory burden. GI cancer, where malnutrition and systemic inflammation are most prevalent~\cite{kapala2025,poisson2021}, was the only malignancy in which all three indices significantly predicted mortality, and showed the most pronounced survival gradient across PNI tertiles (4.8 years between low and high groups). In contrast, breast and prostate/urinary cancers, where nutritional-inflammatory derangement is less common~\cite{poisson2021}, showed attenuated or absent associations. Second, in the pooled cross-cancer analysis of 2,942 patients, PNI, CONUT, and GNRI consistently predicted all-cause mortality (pooled HR $\sim$0.78--0.80, all p$<$0.001) but with significant CONUT $\times$ cancer type interaction (p=0.016), reflecting differential prognostic relevance of cholesterol and BMI across malignancies. Third, the in-depth GI analysis revealed that CONUT and GNRI robustly predict mortality after Bonferroni correction, while PNI shows a nominally significant but Bonferroni-non-significant association that is strongly time-dependent (concentrated in the first 2 years), operates through non-cancer mortality pathways, and is driven by albumin alone.

\subsection{Cross-Cancer Performance and Cancer Type Interaction}

The CONUT $\times$ cancer type interaction is biologically plausible, and the systematic superiority of all three indices in GI cancer is grounded in fundamental differences in nutritional-inflammatory burden across malignancies. Cancer-related malnutrition risk varies dramatically by primary site: up to 80.4\% for GI tumors versus 9.1\% for breast cancer and 4.8\% for prostate cancer~\cite{kapala2025}. The NutriAgeCancer nationwide survey confirmed this gradient, reporting cachexia in 76.4\% of upper GI, 62.4\% of colorectal, 29.3\% of breast, and 50.6\% of prostate cancer patients~\cite{poisson2021}. Cachexia is independently associated with mortality (adjusted HR 1.49)~\cite{poisson2021} and reflects a distinct hypermetabolic syndrome driven by tumor-derived cytokines (IL-6, TNF-$\alpha$) that simultaneously degrade the components measured by nutritional indices: albumin (as a negative acute-phase reactant), lymphocyte count (via immune exhaustion), and cholesterol (via metabolic dysregulation)~\cite{fearon2011}.

Although we did not directly diagnose cachexia (NHANES lacks weight loss and muscle mass data required for the Fearon consensus definition or GLIM criteria), the literature on GI-specific cachexia mechanisms provides a framework for interpreting our findings. The uniquely high malnutrition burden in GI cancer arises from several interconnected mechanisms that distinguish it from non-GI malignancies. First, GI tumors directly impair digestive and absorptive function through anatomical disruption---esophageal and gastric tumors cause dysphagia and early satiety, pancreatic exocrine insufficiency eliminates lipase and protease secretion, and biliary obstruction prevents fat emulsification~\cite{fearon2011}. Second, GI cancers induce profound gut barrier dysfunction (``leaky gut''): tumor-derived IL-6 downregulates tight junction proteins (occludin, claudin-1, ZO-1) in the intestinal epithelium, enabling translocation of bacterial lipopolysaccharide into the portal circulation. This endotoxemia activates Toll-like receptors on hepatic Kupffer cells and circulating macrophages, amplifying TNF-$\alpha$ and IL-6 production in a feed-forward loop that perpetuates catabolism even when caloric intake is maintained~\cite{poisson2021}. Third, GI cancer-associated wasting is increasingly recognized as a dysbiosis-driven syndrome: pathobionts such as enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis and Klebsiella oxytoca expand at the expense of short-chain fatty acid producers (Faecalibacterium, Roseburia, Akkermansia), depleting butyrate that normally protects muscle mass via Akt/mTOR/Foxo3a signaling, while simultaneously reducing circulating branched-chain amino acids needed for muscle protein synthesis~\cite{bindels2016}. This gut-centric inflammatory amplification is absent in breast and prostate cancer, where the intestinal barrier and microbiome remain largely intact, explaining why the nutritional-inflammatory axis is less disrupted and why PNI, CONUT, and GNRI show weaker discrimination in those populations.

This pathophysiological framework explains our cross-cancer findings. In GI malignancies, where systemic inflammation and nutritional depletion dominate the clinical picture, nutritional indices capture the central axis of disease progression and therefore show strong, consistent prognostic associations. In breast cancer, where nutritional-inflammatory derangement affects fewer than one-third of patients~\cite{poisson2021}, indices that rely on cholesterol (CONUT) or BMI (GNRI) are further confounded by estrogen receptor status and hormone therapy effects on lipid profiles~\cite{nelson2014}, rendering them non-significant. In prostate cancer, where the inflammatory-nutritional disruption is less pronounced but the obesity paradox complicates BMI interpretation~\cite{lopez2022}, all three indices showed attenuated associations. Notably, prostate/urinary cancer patients had the highest mean CONUT score (1.36) yet CONUT was non-significant (HR 0.93, p=0.298), suggesting that elevated CONUT in this population reflects age-related multimorbidity rather than malnutrition-specific mortality risk. The strong associations observed in the "other solid tumor" group (e.g., CONUT HR 0.71) should be interpreted cautiously given its biological heterogeneity, as this category pools malignancies with divergent etiologies and prognoses.

The recent Clinical Nutrition study by Renard et al.~\cite{renard2025} further supports this interpretation. In 1,370 patients across tumor types, unsupervised clustering of nutritional and inflammatory parameters identified five universal prognostic clusters independent of tumor origin and metastatic status, with the ``critically impaired/inflamed'' cluster showing nearly 3-fold mortality risk. This cancer-agnostic prognostic value of host factors is consistent with our pooled findings, but the magnitude and precision of nutritional index effects are proportional to the underlying nutritional-inflammatory burden of each cancer type.

Consistent effect estimates were reported by Li et al.~\cite{li2025}, who analyzed an overlapping NHANES cohort (1999--2018) with a similar design and validated their findings in an independent Chinese hospital GI cancer cohort (n=459, Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University), where low GNRI ($<$98) conferred an HR of 1.74 (95\% CI 1.22--2.48) and elevated CONUT ($\ge$2) an HR of 1.89 (95\% CI 1.29--2.77)---comparable to our GI-specific estimates (GNRI HR 0.67 per 1-SD, corresponding to approximately HR 1.49 when inverted; CONUT HR 0.72 per 1-SD). A concurrent umbrella review of 138 meta-analyses~\cite{li2025b} rated PNI, CONUT, and GNRI as Class II (highly suggestive) evidence for predicting oncological survival, but notably found that the optimal tool varied by cancer site---PNI showed the strongest evidence in colorectal and hepatocellular carcinoma, CONUT in gastric and urothelial cancer, and GNRI in gastric cancer and hematologic malignancies---consistent with our demonstration that cancer type moderates index performance. While Li et al.~\cite{li2025} focused on GI-specific validation with external replication, the present study extends these findings by incorporating the NHANES III generation (1988--1994) and providing a simultaneous evaluation of PNI, CONUT, and GNRI with time-dependent and competing-risks decomposition across five cancer types.

PNI showed no significant cross-cancer interaction (p=0.23), though limited statistical power for interaction testing should be noted. This may reflect albumin's universal role as a combined nutritional marker and negative acute-phase reactant, suggesting PNI could serve as a generic screening tool across cancer types, with CONUT added for malnutrition-prone populations where its additional components may provide incremental prognostic information.

Because NHANES surveys participants at varying times after diagnosis (prevalent cohort design), the observed cross-cancer ranking of index performance may partly reflect differential length-biased sampling: patients with aggressive malignancies (e.g., pancreatic cancer) who survive long enough to be included are highly selected survivors, whereas those with indolent cancers (e.g., prostate) are more representative of the full disease spectrum. This should be considered when interpreting cross-cancer comparisons.

\subsection{Time-Dependent and Cause-Specific Mechanisms in GI Cancer}

The temporal and mechanistic dissection in GI cancer requires careful interpretation. PNI's marked time-dependent attenuation---HR 0.34 in the first 2 years, null thereafter---has two equally plausible explanations. It may reflect a single baseline measurement capturing risk in the peri-diagnosis period when treatment tolerance and postoperative recovery dominate~\cite{lee2017}. Alternatively, this pattern is consistent with confounding by disease severity: patients with advanced-stage cancer are simultaneously more malnourished and at highest risk of short-term mortality, and NHANES lacks stage data. The complete attenuation after excluding early deaths (HR 0.98) does not distinguish between these explanations. CONUT and GNRI, incorporating total cholesterol and BMI, showed more stable long-term associations, likely because these components reflect more enduring metabolic characteristics.

The competing risks finding---that PNI protects against non-cancer death (HR 0.63) but not GI cancer death (HR 1.06)---provides mechanistic clarity: these indices capture physiological reserve and resilience to treatment-related complications rather than directly influencing tumor biology. This aligns with ESPEN guidelines emphasizing that nutritional therapy aims to maintain capacity for anti-cancer treatment rather than modify cancer biology per se~\cite{muscaritoli2021,arends2017}. Patients with low PNI may benefit from intensified supportive care and closer monitoring for non-cancer complications, particularly in the early post-diagnosis period.

\subsection{Dual-Cohort Validation: Generational Differences and Consistent Prognosis}

The dual-cohort design revealed substantial demographic and clinical differences between the two NHANES generations (Supplementary Table~S8). Compared with the Continuous NHANES era (2005--2016), GI cancer patients in NHANES III (1988--1994) were older (67.5 vs. 57.5 years), more predominantly male (56.9\% vs. 40.3\%), more likely Non-Hispanic White (68.4\% vs. 32.6\%), and had higher CONUT scores (1.28 vs. 0.69). Tumor site distribution also shifted markedly: colorectal cancer accounted for 79.3\% of GI cancers in the Continuous NHANES era versus 40.5\% in NHANES III, where 30.2\% of GI cancer sites were unspecified. Mortality was substantially higher in NHANES III (78.6\% vs. 48.0\%), primarily reflecting the longer elapsed follow-up time (median 19.1 vs. 7.1 years).

Despite these profound differences in population composition, era of diagnosis, and follow-up duration, no statistically significant heterogeneity was detected across generations (all cohort-by-index interaction p$>$0.05). However, these cohort-stratified results should be interpreted cautiously given the modest sample size within each generation (NHANES III n=126, Continuous n=227), which provides limited statistical power for detecting interaction effects and precludes definitive conclusions about cross-generational consistency. The rising proportion of early-onset colorectal cancer in younger populations during the Continuous NHANES era~\cite{kapala2025}---reflected in the younger age of the Continuous NHANES GI cohort---further underscores the contemporary relevance of nutritional risk stratification, as these younger patients face longer survivorship periods during which nutritional status may influence both cancer-specific and non-cancer outcomes.

\subsection{PNI Decomposition, Inflammation, and Dietary Quality}

PNI decomposition revealed that serum albumin alone outperforms the composite PNI, while lymphocyte count contributes no independent prognostic information (LRT p=0.460). This calls into question whether the lymphocyte component of PNI adds meaningful value beyond albumin in this population. Albumin's dual role as a nutritional marker and negative acute-phase reactant explains its prognostic power, consistent with the substantial overlap between nutritional and inflammatory pathways demonstrated in the mutually adjusted PNI-NLR models.

Inflammatory markers (NLR, SII) showed comparable prognostic performance to PNI (+0.017 C-statistic for both), and the joint Low PNI + High NLR group identified particularly high-risk patients (HR 1.67). The weak correlation between HEI-2015 and PNI (r=0.16, Supplementary Materials) confirms that biochemical nutritional status and dietary intake patterns capture distinct dimensions of nutritional status.

\subsection{Strengths and Limitations}

Key strengths include national representativeness from two independent survey eras spanning 30 years, comprehensive analytical framework (time-dependent Cox, landmark, RCS, RMST, competing risks with both cause-specific and Fine-Gray models, PNI decomposition with bootstrap C-statistics, joint interaction analysis with RERI, and E-value sensitivity), and the integrated cross-cancer comparison enabling assessment of generalizability versus context-specific performance.

Several limitations warrant consideration. Cancer stage, grade, and treatment data---the strongest prognostic factors---are not available in NHANES public-use data, introducing potential confounding by disease severity. Cancer diagnoses are self-reported without registry verification. In NHANES III, 30.2\% of GI cancer cases had unspecified primary sites, and differential misclassification by generation may affect cross-cohort comparisons. A sensitivity analysis excluding unspecified GI cases was examined; results were not materially different (data not shown), though the small sample size limits the informativeness of this exclusion. The public-use mortality file provides only a 10-group cause-of-death recode, limiting cause-specific granularity; the proxy used for GI cancer death (baseline GI cancer plus UCOD=002) may misclassify deaths from non-GI malignancies in GI patients.

The prevalent cohort design is an important methodological constraint. NHANES surveys participants at a single time point after cancer diagnosis, with no data on time since diagnosis. This introduces length-biased sampling (patients with longer survival are overrepresented) and precludes adjustment for disease duration. The observed PNI-mortality association may partly reflect confounding by disease severity, particularly given the absence of reliable cancer staging---sensitivity analyses excluding early deaths partially address reverse causation but cannot fully substitute for incident cohort data. Because length-biased sampling may operate differentially across cancer types (e.g., pancreatic cancer survivors in NHANES are more selected than prostate cancer survivors), its impact on cross-cancer comparisons is uncertain and cross-cancer findings should be interpreted with this caveat in mind. The PNI time-dependent effect (concentrated in the first 2 years) is equally compatible with a true early nutritional effect and with confounding by disease severity, and the available data cannot distinguish these explanations.

Single baseline nutritional measurements are an additional constraint. The GI analysis is predominantly colorectal (67\%), limiting generalizability to upper GI sites. The ``other solid tumor'' category is heterogeneous, encompassing biologically distinct malignancies (melanoma, thyroid, brain, head/neck, sarcoma), and results from this group should be interpreted as a summary of association across diverse tumor biologies rather than evidence for any single site. GNRI was originally validated in elderly populations ($\ge$65 years), and its application to younger cancer types (e.g., female reproductive, mean age 57) may not carry the same prognostic meaning. The prior GI paper filtering to age $\ge$60 is not applied here; sensitivity analyses using age $\ge$60 in the GI subset (n=313) yielded consistent results (data not shown). The PNI threshold of 48.5 was identified via maximally selected log-rank statistics using the Lausen--Schumacher asymptotic correction for multiple cutpoint testing. The threshold requires independent validation, and the RMST and PAF estimates derived from it should be interpreted conservatively. The survey-weighted analysis attenuated PNI's GI-specific association (HR 0.87, p=0.430), suggesting caution in interpreting this finding. We also acknowledge that the non-significant interaction tests for PNI and GNRI across cancer types (both p=0.23) may reflect limited statistical power given the modest sample sizes within each cancer type, rather than true consistency of effects.

\subsection{Clinical Implications}

These simple, inexpensive indices, calculable from routine laboratory data in any clinical setting, align with ESPEN recommendations for routine nutritional screening from the time of cancer diagnosis~\cite{muscaritoli2021}. Within the Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition (GLIM) framework---the current international consensus for malnutrition diagnosis with over 2,500 citations~\cite{cederholm2019}---low values of PNI, CONUT, or GNRI may serve as screening triggers that warrant formal GLIM assessment, though they do not substitute for the full phenotypic and etiologic criteria. A recent systematic review of 28,726 cancer patients confirmed that GLIM-defined malnutrition consistently predicted decreased survival across 18 of 21 studies~\cite{brown2023}, and a large multicenter prospective study (n=4,769) further validated the prognostic performance of GLIM criteria using simple anthropometric measures~\cite{xie2024}. A PNI threshold of 48.5 identifies a high-risk GI subgroup with a 1.97-year RMST difference at 10 years (PAF 13.4\%). The concentration of risk in the first 2 years suggests that early nutritional evaluation has the greatest potential impact. CONUT and GNRI, showing stable long-term associations, may be preferable for extended risk stratification. These data suggest PNI warrants further evaluation as a cross-cancer screening tool, bearing in mind that its GI-specific performance was time-dependent, driven by non-cancer mortality, and attenuated in survey-weighted analysis. CONUT may be preferred for malnutrition-prone malignancies where its additional components provide incremental prognostic information. However, this algorithm was not prospectively tested in this study and requires external validation before clinical adoption.

\section{Conclusions}

PNI, CONUT, and GNRI predict all-cause mortality across multiple cancer types, but their discriminative power varies in a pattern consistent with the underlying nutritional-inflammatory burden. GI cancer, where malnutrition affects the majority of patients, showed the widest survival stratification, with CONUT demonstrating the most robust association after survey-weighted analysis. The PNI association was time-dependent and driven by non-cancer mortality, and its modest E-values caution against causal interpretation without adjustment for cancer stage. These simple, inexpensive indices may facilitate early nutritional risk stratification, though prospective validation and decision-analytic evaluation are needed before clinical implementation.
